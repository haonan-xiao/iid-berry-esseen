\begin{abstract}
We prove that the absolute constant in the classical Berry--Esseen inequality
for identically distributed summands is at most \(0.45\).  The key quantity is
the symmetrized third-moment ratio
\(r=\E\abs{X-X'}^3/(2\E\abs X^3)\), where \(X'\) is an independent copy of a
centered variance-one summand \(X\).
Symmetrization yields a modulus bound at scale \(r\E\abs X^3\), while the same
\(r\) sharpens the third-order bound for the imaginary part of the
characteristic function.  Retaining this dependence gives
a sharper single-summand comparison with the Gaussian characteristic function.
Prawitz smoothing then reduces the proof to a scalar inequality on a compact
parameter domain, which is established by exact dyadic interval certificates.
The analytic reduction and the exact certificate are formally verified in
Lean.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Let \(X,X_1,X_2,\ldots\) be independent and identically distributed real
random variables with
\[
  \E X=0,\qquad \E X^2=1,\qquad
  \rho:=\E\abs{X}^3<\infty,
\]
and call a law satisfying these conditions admissible.  Set
\[
  S_n:=\frac{X_1+\cdots+X_n}{\sqrt n},\qquad
  \Delta_n:=\sup_{x\in\R}\abs{\Pp\{S_n\le x\}-\Phi(x)},
\]
where \(\Phi\) denotes the standard normal distribution function.  The
optimal i.i.d. Berry--Esseen constant is
\[
 C_0:=\inf\left\{C>0:\Delta_n\le
 C\frac{\rho}{\sqrt n}\text{ for all admissible laws and all }n\ge1\right\}.
\]
Esseen's classical example gives
\[
 C_0\ge C_{\mathrm E}:=
 \frac{\sqrt{10}+3}{6\sqrt{2\pi}}
 =0.409732\ldots\,\cite{esseen1956}.
\]
Shevtsova proved the published upper bound \(C_0\le0.4690\)
\cite{shevtsova2013}; Bobkov's survey records the resulting interval
\(0.4097<C_0<0.4690\) \cite[Theorem~20.1]{bobkov2016}.
To the best of our knowledge, \(0.4690\) is the smallest previously published
general i.i.d. upper bound.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
For every admissible law and every integer \(n\ge1\),
\[
  \Delta_n\le 0.45\,\frac{\rho}{\sqrt n}.
\]
Consequently, \(C_0\le0.45\).
\end{theorem}

The improvement comes from retaining the dependence between two estimates for
the characteristic function.  If \(X'\) is an independent copy of \(X\), set
\[
 r:=\frac{\E\abs{X-X'}^3}{2\rho}.
\]
The symmetrized law gives a modulus bound depending on \(r\rho\).  A stop-loss
identity, applied to the same ratio, bounds the sine remainder
\(\E(\sin(uX)-uX)\), equivalently the imaginary part of
\(\E e^{iuX}-(1-u^2/2)\).  The modulus bound weakens as \(r\) increases,
whereas the bound on the sine remainder strengthens.  Preserving this
dependence sharpens the comparison between \(\E e^{iuX}\) and \(e^{-u^2/2}\).

Write \(L=\rho/\sqrt n\).  A direct estimate handles \(L\ge56/45\).  For
\(L<56/45\), Prawitz's smoothing inequality turns these
characteristic-function bounds into an explicit scalar inequality in
\((n,\rho,r)\).  Exact interval arithmetic proves it on the full continuous
parameter domain.

\section{Moment constraints for the characteristic function}%
\label{sec:moment-parameter}

Two pointwise estimates will be needed below: a modulus bound obtained from
\(X-X'\), and a bound on the sine remainder \(\E(\sin(uX)-uX)\).  Both depend
on the ratio \(r\).  We first determine its admissible range and the
corresponding restriction on the sine remainder.

\subsection{The symmetrized third-moment ratio}\label{sec:moment-geometry}

Let \(X'\) be an independent copy of \(X\), and define
\[
 m:=\E\abs{X-X'}^3,\qquad r:=\frac{m}{2\rho}.
\]
For \(t\ge0\), introduce the stop-loss functions
\[
 A(t):=\E(X-t)_+,\qquad B(t):=\E(-X-t)_+,
\]
and put \(r_0:=\E X_+=\E X_-\).

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:stoploss}
One has
\begin{align}
 m&=2\rho+12\int_0^\infty\bigl(A(t)^2+B(t)^2\bigr)\dd t,
 \label{eq:m-stop}\tag{2.1}\\
 3\int_0^\infty A(t)^2\dd t&\le r_0\E X_+^2,
 \qquad
 3\int_0^\infty B(t)^2\dd t\le r_0\E X_-^2.
 \label{eq:hardy}\tag{2.2}
\end{align}
Consequently,
\[
 1\le r\le1+\frac{\E\abs X}{\rho}
 \le1+\frac1\rho\le2.
 \label{eq:r-range}\tag{2.3}
\]
If \(\mu:=\E X^3\), then
\[
 (r-1)^2+\left(\frac\mu\rho\right)^2\le1.
 \label{eq:moment-circle}\tag{2.4}
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For real \(x,y\),
\[
 \abs{x-y}^3=6\int_{\R}
 \bigl[(x-t)_+(t-y)_+ +(y-t)_+(t-x)_+\bigr]\dd t.
\]
Taking expectations, using independence, splitting the integral at zero,
and applying \(\E X=0\) gives
\[
 m=12\int_0^\infty\bigl(A^2+B^2+tA+tB\bigr)\dd t.
\]
Fubini's theorem gives
\(\int_0^\infty tA(t)\dd t=\E X_+^3/6\) and the analogous identity
for \(B\), proving \eqref{eq:m-stop}.

If \(0\le y\le z\), direct integration gives
\[
 3\int_0^\infty(y-t)_+(z-t)_+\dd t
 =\frac32y^2z-\frac12y^3
 \le\frac12(yz^2+y^2z).
\]
Averaging independent copies proves \eqref{eq:hardy}.  Since
\(\E X_+^2+\E X_-^2=1\), it follows that
\(m-2\rho\le4r_0=2\E\abs X\), and \eqref{eq:r-range} follows from
\(\E\abs X\le1\) and Lyapunov's inequality \(\rho\ge1\).

To prove \eqref{eq:moment-circle}, write
\[
 a_j:=\E X_+^j,\qquad b_j:=\E X_-^j\quad(j=2,3).
\]
Positive semidefiniteness of the covariance matrix of \((X_+,X_-)\),
together with \(X_+X_-=0\), gives \(a_2b_2\ge r_0^2\).
Cauchy's inequality gives \(a_2^2\le r_0a_3\) and
\(b_2^2\le r_0b_3\), hence \(r_0\le\sqrt{a_3b_3}\).  Therefore
\[
 m-2\rho\le4\sqrt{a_3b_3}
 =2\sqrt{\rho^2-\mu^2}.
\]
Division by \(2\rho\) proves \eqref{eq:moment-circle}.
\end{proof}

The range in \eqref{eq:r-range} sets the scale in the modulus estimate.  At the
same time, \eqref{eq:moment-circle} shows that a larger value of \(r-1\) forces
the normalized third moment to be smaller.  The next result transfers this
restriction to the sine remainder at every frequency.

\subsection{A bound for the sine remainder}

Since \(\E X=0\), the imaginary part of
\(\E e^{iuX}-(1-u^2/2)\) is \(\E(\sin(uX)-uX)\).  The same ratio \(r\)
controls this quantity uniformly in \(u\).

\begin{lemma}[Bound for the sine remainder]\label{lem:sine-circle}
For every \(u\in\R\),
\[
 \abs{\E(\sin(uX)-uX)}
 \le\frac{\rho\abs u^3}{6}\sqrt{1-(r-1)^2}.
 \label{eq:sine-circle}\tag{2.5}
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
It is enough to take \(u>0\).  Define
\[
 J:=\int_0^\infty t(A+B)\dd t=\frac\rho6,
 \quad E_0:=\int_0^\infty(A^2+B^2)\dd t,
 \quad H_u:=\frac1u\int_0^\infty\sin(ut)(A-B)\dd t.
\]
For a centered two-point law, these normalized quantities satisfy
\(E_0^2+H_u^2\le J^2\).  Stop-loss functions are affine under mixtures, so
Minkowski's inequality carries the same constraint to an arbitrary centered
law.  We now make the mixture explicit.

Let \(\alpha_+\) be the restriction
of the law of \(X\) to \((0,\infty)\), and let \(\alpha_-\) be the reflection
of its restriction to \(( -\infty,0)\).  Centering gives
\[
 \int a\,\alpha_+(\dd a)=\int b\,\alpha_-(\dd b)=r_0,
\]
and the variance-one assumption implies \(r_0>0\).
On \((0,\infty)^2\), define
\[
 \nu(\dd a,\dd b):=\frac{a+b}{r_0}\,
 \alpha_+(\dd a)\alpha_-(\dd b),
\]
and add mass \(\Pp\{X=0\}\) at a zero component.  For each \((a,b)\), let
\(X_{a,b}\) take the values \(a\) and \(-b\) with probabilities
\(b/(a+b)\) and \(a/(a+b)\).  A direct calculation shows that the law of
\(X\) is the \(\nu\)-mixture of these centered two-point laws and the zero
law.  In particular, their stop-loss functions mix linearly to \((A,B)\).

For one such component, let \(J_{a,b},E_{a,b},H_{a,b}\) denote the three
quantities above, and put \(x=ua\), \(y=ub\).  Direct calculation gives
\[
 \frac{E_{a,b}}{J_{a,b}}=\frac{2xy}{x^2+y^2},\qquad
 \frac{H_{a,b}}{J_{a,b}}
 =-\frac{6(y\sin x-x\sin y)}{xy(x^2+y^2)}.
\]
For \(z>0\),
\[
 \left|\frac{\dd}{\dd(z^2)}\frac{\sin z}{z}\right|
 =\frac{\abs{z\cos z-\sin z}}{2z^3}\le\frac16,
\]
because
\[
 \abs{z\cos z-\sin z}
 =\left|\int_0^z t\sin t\dd t\right|
 \le\int_0^z t^2\dd t=\frac{z^3}{3}.
\]
It follows that \(E_{a,b}^2+H_{a,b}^2\le J_{a,b}^2\).  Jensen's inequality
applied at each \(t\), together with linearity of the mixture, gives
\[
 E_0\le\int E_{a,b}\nu(\dd a,\dd b),\qquad
 H_u=\int H_{a,b}\nu(\dd a,\dd b),\qquad
 J=\int J_{a,b}\nu(\dd a,\dd b).
\]
Minkowski's inequality in \(\R^2\) now proves
\[
 \sqrt{E_0^2+H_u^2}
 \le\int\sqrt{E_{a,b}^2+H_{a,b}^2}\nu(\dd a,\dd b)
 \le J.
\]

Two integrations of the sine remainder yield
\(\E(uX-\sin(uX))=u^3H_u\).  By \eqref{eq:m-stop},
\(E_0/J=r-1\).  Thus \(E_0^2+H_u^2\le J^2\) gives
\(\abs{H_u}\le(\rho/6)\sqrt{1-(r-1)^2}\), proving
\eqref{eq:sine-circle}.
\end{proof}

Thus the symmetrized third-moment ratio governs both quantities needed below:
it sets the scale of the modulus estimate and sharpens the bound on the sine
remainder.

\section{Comparison with the Gaussian characteristic function}%
\label{sec:cf-bounds}

Let \(f(u):=\E e^{iuX}\).  At each frequency, the smoothing argument will use
both a bound for \(\abs{f(u)}\) and a bound for
\(\abs{f(u)-e^{-u^2/2}}\).  We derive both in terms of the same ratio \(r\).

For the modulus bound, put
\[
 g(v):=\cos v-1+\frac{v^2}{2},\qquad
 \kappa:=\max_{v>0}\frac{g(v)}{v^3}.
\]
Exact alternating-series bounds and interval Newton verification give
\[
 0.09916191350<\kappa<0.09916191353
\]
and a unique maximizer \(\vartheta\) satisfying
\[
 3.99589567<\vartheta<3.99589570.
\]
We use Prawitz's piecewise characteristic-function minorant
\cite{prawitz1975cf}, retaining the scale \(r\rho\) controlled in Section~2:
\[
 q(v):=
 \begin{cases}
 \frac12-\kappa v,&0\le v\le\vartheta,\\[1mm]
 \dfrac{1-\cos v}{v^2},&\vartheta<v\le2\pi,\\[2mm]
 0,&v>2\pi.
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:q-def}\tag{3.1}
\]

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:q}
The function \(q\) is convex and nonincreasing, and
\[
 q(v)\le\frac{1-\cos v}{v^2}\qquad(v>0).
\]
Consequently,
\[
 \abs{f(u)}^2\le1-2u^2q(r\rho\abs u).
 \label{eq:modulus}\tag{3.2}
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The definition of \(\kappa\) gives the minorant on the first interval.
At \(\vartheta\), equality and equality of the first derivatives hold.
For \(\psi(v)=(1-\cos v)/v^2\),
\[
 \psi''(v)=\frac{v^2\cos v-4v\sin v+6(1-\cos v)}{v^4}.
\]
The numerator is nonnegative on \([\vartheta,2\pi]\), and
\(\psi'(2\pi)=0\).  The required breakpoint and sign statements are verified
exactly in the Lean development.  Hence the three pieces join with
nondecreasing slopes and \(q\) is convex and nonincreasing.

Let \(Y=X-X'\).  Since \(\E Y^2=2\), the measure \(Y^2\dd\Pp/2\) is a
probability measure.  Jensen's inequality gives
\begin{align*}
 1-\abs{f(u)}^2
 &=\E(1-\cos(uY))\\
 &\ge2u^2q\left(\abs u\frac{\E\abs Y^3}{\E Y^2}\right)
 =2u^2q(r\rho\abs u),
\end{align*}
which is \eqref{eq:modulus}.
\end{proof}

We next bound the difference from the Gaussian characteristic function.
After normalization by \(\rho\abs u^3\), the ordinary third-order Taylor bound
places the remainder in a disk of radius \(1/6\), while
Lemma~\ref{lem:sine-circle} restricts its imaginary part according to \(r\).
The corresponding normalized Gaussian correction lies on the real axis, so
the largest possible distance is a two-dimensional constrained maximum.  Set
\[
 \beta(r):=\frac16\sqrt{1-(r-1)^2},\qquad d_0:=\frac16,
\]
and, for \(c\ge0\), define
\[
 \epsilon_\rho(c):=
 \begin{cases}
 \displaystyle\frac{\rho^2}{c^3}
 \left(e^{-c^2/(2\rho^2)}-1+\frac{c^2}{2\rho^2}\right),&c>0,\\[2mm]
 0,&c=0.
 \end{cases}
\]
Let
\[
 \Xi(r):=\{0,\kappa\}\cup
 \left(\left\{\frac{r-1}{6}\right\}\cap[0,\kappa]\right)
\]
and
\[
 D(\rho,r,c)^2:=\max_{x\in\Xi(r)}
 \left\{(x-\epsilon_\rho(c))^2+
 \min\bigl(\beta(r)^2,d_0^2-x^2\bigr)\right\}.
 \label{eq:D-def}\tag{3.3}
\]
Thus \(D(\rho,r,c)\) is the greatest possible distance from the normalized
Gaussian correction \(\epsilon_\rho(c)\) to a Taylor remainder satisfying the
disk and imaginary-part bounds; \(\Xi(r)\) lists the only real parts at which
the maximum can occur.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:one-step}
For every \(u\in\R\), with \(c=\rho\abs u\),
\[
 \abs{f(u)-e^{-u^2/2}}\le\rho\abs u^3D(\rho,r,c).
 \label{eq:one-step}\tag{3.4}
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The case \(u=0\) is immediate.  Suppose \(u\ne0\), and set
\[
 z:=\frac{f(u)-(1-u^2/2)}{\rho\abs u^3},
\]
the definition of \(\kappa\), Lemma~\ref{lem:sine-circle}, and the
third-order Taylor remainder give
\[
 0\le\Re z\le\kappa,\qquad
 \abs{\Im z}\le\beta(r),\qquad \abs z\le d_0.
\]
Moreover,
\[
 \frac{e^{-u^2/2}-(1-u^2/2)}{\rho\abs u^3}
 =\epsilon_\rho(c).
\]
Thus the normalized left side of \eqref{eq:one-step} is the distance
from the nonnegative real point \(\epsilon_\rho(c)\) to the intersection
of a vertical strip, a horizontal strip, and the disk of radius \(d_0\).
For fixed real part \(x\), the largest squared imaginary part is
\(\min(\beta(r)^2,d_0^2-x^2)\).  The squared distance can change form
only at \(x=(r-1)/6\), and its maximum on each piece is attained at an
endpoint.  These are exactly the points in \(\Xi(r)\), proving
\eqref{eq:D-def} and \eqref{eq:one-step}.
\end{proof}

The two estimates needed for smoothing are now explicit:
\eqref{eq:modulus} bounds the modulus, and \eqref{eq:one-step} bounds the
deviation from the Gaussian, with their dependence on the same \(r\)
preserved.

\section{Prawitz smoothing and the
\texorpdfstring{\(0.45\)}{0.45} bound}\label{sec:prawitz}

Set \(L:=\rho/\sqrt n\).  When \(L\) is large, a distribution-free
Kolmogorov bound is sufficient.  When \(L\) is smaller, Prawitz smoothing
turns the two characteristic-function estimates from
Section~\ref{sec:cf-bounds} into the desired distributional bound.  We begin
with the large-\(L\) regime.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:universal}
If \(W\) is centered and has variance one, then
\[
  \sup_x\abs{\Pp\{W\le x\}-\Phi(x)}\le\frac{14}{25}=0.56.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Cantelli's inequality gives, for \(a\ge0\),
\[
 \Pp\{W\ge a\}\le\frac1{1+a^2},\qquad
 \Pp\{W\le-a\}\le\frac1{1+a^2}.
\]
Hence the Kolmogorov distance is at most
\[
 \max\left\{\frac12,\sup_{a\ge0}
 \left(\frac1{1+a^2}-\Phi(-a)\right)\right\}.
\]
For \(a\ge1\), the second term is at most \(1/2\).  For
\(0\le a\le1\), the bound \((2\pi)^{-1/2}<2/5\) yields
\[
 \frac1{1+a^2}-\Phi(-a)
 \le \frac1{1+a^2}-\frac12+\frac{2a}{5}.
\]
The last expression is at most \(14/25\) exactly when
\[
 3-20a+53a^2-20a^3\ge0.
\]
On \([0,1/4]\) the left side is at least
\(3-20a+48a^2>0\), and on \([1/4,1/2]\) it is at least
\(3-20a+43a^2>0\).
On \([1/2,1]\), write the polynomial as
\(3+a(-20a^2+53a-20)\); the concave quadratic in parentheses is positive
at both endpoints.  This proves the claim.
\end{proof}

Applied to \(W=S_n\), Lemma~\ref{lem:universal} proves
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} whenever \(L\ge56/45\), since
\(0.56=0.45(56/45)\).  It remains to consider
\[
  1\le\rho<\frac{56}{45}\sqrt n.
\]

For this range we use the following form of the Prawitz smoothing inequality.
For \(0<t<1\), define the Prawitz kernel
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 K(t)&:=\frac{1-t}{2}
 +\frac{i}{2}\left((1-t)\cot(\pi t)+\frac1\pi\right),\\
 K(-t)&:=\overline{K(t)}.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:kernel}\tag{4.1}
\]
The following normalization of the Prawitz smoothing inequality converts
characteristic-function estimates into a bound on Kolmogorov distance
\cite{prawitz1972}.  It is equivalent to the filter normalization recorded in
\cite[Section~2]{pinelis2013}.

\begin{lemma}[Prawitz smoothing inequality]\label{lem:prawitz}
Let \(Y\) be an integrable real random variable, with distribution function
\(F\) and characteristic function \(h\).
For \(T>0\) and \(0<t_0<1\),
\begin{align}
 \sup_x\abs{F(x)-\Phi(x)}
 \le{}&2\int_0^{t_0}\abs{K(t)}
       \abs{h(Tt)-e^{-T^2t^2/2}}\dd t \notag\\
 &+2\int_{t_0}^1\abs{K(t)}\abs{h(Tt)}\dd t \notag\\
 &+2\int_0^{t_0}\left|K(t)-\frac{i}{2\pi t}\right|
       e^{-T^2t^2/2}\dd t \notag\\
 &+\frac1\pi\int_{t_0}^{\infty}e^{-T^2t^2/2}\frac{\dd t}{t}.
 \label{eq:prawitz}\tag{4.2}
\end{align}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let \(J(x)=(\sin(\pi x)/(\pi x))^2\), with its removable value at zero,
and let \(H\) be the odd band-limited function in Prawitz's construction.
The pointwise estimate
\(\abs{\operatorname{sgn}(x)-H(x)}\le J(x)\) gives
\[
 B_-(x):=\frac{1-H(x)-J(x)}2
 \le \mathbf 1_{(-\infty,0]}(x)
 \le \frac{1-H(x)+J(x)}2=:B_+(x).
\]
The nonconstant Fourier transforms of \(B_+\) and \(B_-\) are supported on
\([-1,1]\); under the normalization in \eqref{eq:kernel}, they are \(K(t)\)
and \(-K(-t)\), respectively.

Apply these bounds to \(T(Y-x)/(2\pi)\), take expectations, and use Fourier
inversion.  The finite first moment removes the apparent singularity at zero:
\[
 \abs{h(u)-1}\le \abs u\,\E\abs Y,
\]
so the zero-frequency constant may be subtracted before passing to the
symmetric Fourier limit.  Subtracting the exact normal inversion formula,
splitting the frequencies at \(t_0\), and using conjugate symmetry gives the
first three terms in \eqref{eq:prawitz}; the remaining normal frequencies give
the last integral.  Applying the same argument to \(-Y\) supplies the opposite
CDF inequality and completes the absolute-value bound.
\end{proof}

We apply Lemma~\ref{lem:prawitz} to the law of \(S_n\), which is integrable
under the third-moment assumption.  With
\(L=\rho/\sqrt n\) as above, choose
\[
 T:=\frac{2\pi}{rL},\qquad t_0:=\frac{19}{50}.
 \label{eq:parameters}\tag{4.3}
\]
For \(0\le t\le1\), set
\begin{align*}
 u(t)&:=\frac{2\pi t}{r\rho},\\
 a(t)&:=\pos{1-2u(t)^2q(2\pi t)}^{1/2},
 &b(t)&:=e^{-u(t)^2/2},\\
 M(t)&:=a(t)^n,
 &N(t)&:=b(t)^n=e^{-T^2t^2/2},
\end{align*}
and
\[
R(t):=\min\left\{
 n\rho u(t)^3D\left(\rho,r,\frac{2\pi t}{r}\right)
 \max(a(t),b(t))^{n-1},\ M(t)+N(t)\right\}.
\]
Here \(a(t)\) and \(b(t)\) are the single-summand characteristic-function and
Gaussian bounds; \(M(t)\) and \(N(t)\) are their \(n\)-fold counterparts.  The
two entries in the minimum defining \(R(t)\) are, respectively, the bound
obtained from the difference-of-powers identity and the triangle-inequality
bound.
With these bounds, the right-hand side of \eqref{eq:prawitz} becomes
\begin{align}
 \mathcal U(n,\rho,r):={}&
 2\int_0^{t_0}\abs{K(t)}R(t)\dd t
 +2\int_{t_0}^1\abs{K(t)}M(t)\dd t \notag\\
 &+2\int_0^{t_0}\left|K(t)-\frac{i}{2\pi t}\right|N(t)\dd t
 +\frac1\pi\int_{t_0}^{\infty}N(t)\frac{\dd t}{t}.
 \label{eq:U-def}\tag{4.4}
\end{align}

\begin{lemma}[Exact scalar bound]\label{lem:numerical}
For every integer \(n\ge1\), every
\[
 1\le\rho\le\frac{56}{45}\sqrt n,
 \qquad 1\le r\le1+\frac1\rho,
 \label{eq:num-domain}\tag{4.5}
\]
one has
\[
 \mathcal U(n,\rho,r)\le0.4495\,\frac\rho{\sqrt n}.
 \label{eq:num-bound}\tag{4.6}
\]
\end{lemma}

This is the only exhaustive computer-assisted step.  Section~\ref{sec:certificate}
proves it by exact interval arithmetic over the entire domain
\eqref{eq:num-domain}.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
If \(L\ge56/45\), Lemma~\ref{lem:universal} gives
\(\Delta_n\le0.56\le0.45L\).  Suppose \(L<56/45\).  Then
Lemma~\ref{lem:stoploss} places \((n,\rho,r)\) in
\eqref{eq:num-domain}.

The characteristic function of \(S_n\) is
\(f_n(t)=f(t/\sqrt n)^n\).  With the choices in
\eqref{eq:parameters}, Lemma~\ref{lem:q} gives
\[
 \abs{f(u(t))}\le a(t),\qquad \abs{f_n(Tt)}\le M(t).
\]
Lemma~\ref{lem:one-step} and
\(\abs{z^n-w^n}\le n\abs{z-w}\max(\abs z,\abs w)^{n-1}\) give
the first bound in the definition of \(R(t)\); the triangle inequality
gives the second.  Lemma~\ref{lem:prawitz} therefore yields
\[
 \Delta_n\le\mathcal U(n,\rho,r).
\]
Lemma~\ref{lem:numerical} now gives
\[
 \Delta_n\le0.4495L<0.45L,
\]
which completes the proof.
\end{proof}

\section{Exact verification of the scalar inequality}\label{sec:certificate}

Lemma~\ref{lem:numerical} is proved by a finite exact certificate.  The
certificate represents a cover of the continuous parameter domain, and a
proved-sound interval checker establishes the required upper bound on every
cell of that cover.

\subsection{Exact interval bounds}

All numerical endpoints are dyadic rationals at precision \(2^{-48}\).
Addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, squaring, nonnegative square
root, and integer powers use proved outward-rounding rules.  Alternating Taylor
polynomials of degrees \(24\) and \(25\), together with exact range reduction,
enclose the exponential and trigonometric functions used by the checker.
The exponential-integral tail is bounded by a convex composite trapezoid rule
on \([0,24]\) and a proved infinite-tail estimate.

Every integral in \eqref{eq:U-def} is replaced by an upper Darboux sum.  Lean
proves the generic implication
\[
 \text{cellwise interval containment}
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 \int_J F(t)\dd t\le
 \sum_{I\in\mathcal P}\abs I\,\sup [F](I),
\]
including the outward-rounded accumulation used by the interval checker.
Removable singularities at \(t=0\) and \(t=1\) are handled algebraically before
interval evaluation.

\subsection{Covering the continuous domain}

For each \(1\le n<100\), the parameter domain is mapped to the unit square by
\[
 \rho=1+y\left(\frac{56}{45}\sqrt n-1\right),
 \qquad r=1+\frac z\rho,
 \qquad 0\le y,z\le1.
\]
A prefix code describes a binary subdivision tree.  A leaf is accepted only
when the exact interval upper bound is below \(0.4495\).  Lean proves that an
accepted tree covers the entire square and that every represented point
satisfies \eqref{eq:num-bound}.

For \(n\ge100\), write \(L=\rho/\sqrt n\).  The bounds
\(a(t),b(t)\le e^{-u(t)^2q(2\pi t)}\), together with
\((n-1)/n\ge99/100\), give
\[
 \max(a(t),b(t))^{n-1}
 \le \exp\left\{-\frac{99}{100}
 \frac{(2\pi t)^2q(2\pi t)}{r^2L^2}\right\}.
\]
This removes the explicit \(n\)-dependence from the telescoping term.
Monotonicity and convexity reduce the remaining dependence on \(\rho\).  The
checker covers three compact regions: \(0\le L\le1/16\), \(1\le r\le2\);
\(1/16\le L\le1/10\), \(1\le r\le2\); and
\(1/10\le L\le56/45\), \(1\le r\le1+(10L)^{-1}\).  Since \(\rho\ge1\),
\(n\ge100\), and \(r\le1+1/\rho\), their union contains every admissible pair.
The endpoint \(L=0\) uses a proved removable-limit formula.  Checker soundness
then gives \eqref{eq:num-bound} throughout each accepted covering tree.

In the upper region, the interval parameter map is intersected with the proved
constraint \(r\le2\) before its tree is applied, so the endpoint is covered
without enlarging the admissible domain.

The complete Lean source and the exact certificate data are available at
\href{https://github.com/haonan-xiao/iid-berry-esseen}%
{\nolinkurl{github.com/haonan-xiao/iid-berry-esseen}}.

The probability-theoretic reduction, moment identities,
characteristic-function estimates, smoothing argument, checker soundness, and
covering theorems are formalized in Lean~4 \cite{demoura2021lean4} on top of
Mathlib \cite{mathlib2020}.  Only the prefix codes, not the program that
searches for them, enter the proof.  Lean recomputes every accepted closed
Boolean certificate with \texttt{native\_decide}.  The preceding formal
arguments are kernel-checked; certificate evaluation additionally trusts
Lean's native compiler through the axioms generated by
\texttt{native\_decide}.  The source contains no unproved placeholders or
user-declared axioms.

This proves Lemma~\ref{lem:numerical} on the entire admissible domain and hence
completes the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.  The role of the certificate is
precise: it verifies the final uniform scalar inequality.  The dependence on
\(r\) that produces the improved bound enters earlier through
\eqref{eq:modulus} and \eqref{eq:one-step}.
